# Patterns & Techniques — Made to Stick

**Commander’s Intent (CI)**
*   **When to use:** When you need to align the behavior of a team in a noisy, unpredictable, or complex environment where detailed, step-by-step instructions would quickly become obsolete [1-3].
*   **How it works:** Strip your plan down to its absolute core by discarding all tangential goals. Identify the **single most important goal** and communicate it as a crisp, plain-talk statement. This empowers individuals to improvise and generate their own solutions while still pursuing the ultimate desired end-state [2-4].
*   **Trade-offs:** Requires **"forced prioritization,"** which is painful because it means sacrificing nuance, complexity, and other beneficial goals [5, 6]. 

**The Inverted Pyramid**
*   **When to use:** When communicating information to an audience with varying attention spans, or when you need to avoid "burying the lead" under mountains of detail [6, 7].
*   **How it works:** Place the **most essential information (the lead) at the very top** of your communication. Present all supporting details in decreasing order of importance, ensuring that if the audience stops reading, they have already absorbed the core point [6-8].
*   **Trade-offs:** It **ruins suspense and narrative payoffs**; readers who break off early or read to the end won't experience a dramatic, mystery-style resolution [7].

**Generative Analogies and Schemas**
*   **When to use:** When you need to rapidly teach a complex or entirely new concept to a novice, or establish a platform for novel, unscripted behavior [9-12].
*   **How it works:** Substitute something easy to think about for something difficult by **tapping into the audience's existing memory structures ("schemas")**. For example, explaining a pomelo as a "supersized grapefruit" or Disney calling its employees "cast members" to enforce a standard of theatrical performance [9, 12-14].
*   **Trade-offs:** The analogy is **only effective if the audience actually shares the underlying schema** [12, 15]. It can also backfire if the analogy maps incorrectly to the desired action (e.g., calling sandwich makers "artists" when they have no creative liberty) [16].

**High-Concept Pitches**
*   **When to use:** To quickly establish common ground, accelerate learning, and guide complex decision-making for a brand-new project or product [17, 18].
*   **How it works:** Create a **compact, proverbial phrase** that relies on existing schemas to pack immense meaning into a new idea (e.g., pitching the movie *Alien* as "*Jaws* on a spaceship") [17-19].
*   **Trade-offs:** A **bad pitch or analogy is enough to ruin a project** by misguiding all subsequent decisions and designs [18].

**The Gap Theory of Curiosity (News-Teaser Approach)**
*   **When to use:** To capture and sustain audience interest over a long period, or to convince people they need your message [20-22].
*   **How it works:** Highlight a specific **gap in the audience's knowledge *before* giving them the facts**. Pose a puzzle, mystery, or "teaser" question that creates a mental "itch," making them want to stay engaged to find the answer [21-23].
*   **Trade-offs:** It **fails if the audience is overconfident** and does not realize they have a knowledge gap [24, 25].

**Combating Overconfidence (Forced Prediction)**
*   **When to use:** When an audience thinks they already know the answer, rendering the Gap Theory ineffective [24, 25].
*   **How it works:** Force the audience to **publicly commit to a prediction or answer**, or show them that others disagree with them. Once they commit, pull the rug out by revealing the unexpected truth to expose their defective schema [25-27].
*   **Trade-offs:** Requires the audience to confront their own ignorance, which can **temporarily induce frustration or discomfort** before generating insight [26, 28].

**Computing in Context**
*   **When to use:** When teaching abstract concepts or formulas to novices [29-31].
*   **How it works:** Explain abstract principles by **anchoring them in concrete, sensory, and familiar real-world situations** (e.g., teaching subtraction using physical tiles or accounting via a semester-long startup business simulation) [29, 32].
*   **Trade-offs:** It **requires significantly more time to set up the context** and simulation than simply delivering abstract facts or formulas [33].

**The Sinatra Test**
*   **When to use:** To establish massive internal credibility when you lack external authority or face extreme skepticism [34-36].
*   **How it works:** Present a single, **extraordinarily difficult, high-profile example of your success** that proves your capability across the board (e.g., "If I can make it there, I'll make it anywhere") [34, 36].
*   **Trade-offs:** You must **possess an extreme, unassailable success story**; without an ironclad "ultimate test," the technique cannot be used [37, 38].

**Testable Credentials**
*   **When to use:** To make a claim highly believable by outsourcing the credibility check directly to your audience [36, 39].
*   **How it works:** Issue a specific challenge that **allows the audience to verify your claim using their own intuition, memory, or physical senses** as a "try before you buy" proposition (e.g., Wendy's "Where's the beef?" campaign asking consumers to look at competitors' burgers) [36, 39, 40].
*   **Trade-offs:** The approach can backfire if the audience tests the credential but **leaps to an illogical or incorrect conclusion** (e.g., assuming a "K" for Kosher on a Snapple bottle stands for the Ku Klux Klan) [41, 42].

**The Human-Scale Principle**
*   **When to use:** To make massive numbers, abstract statistics, or dry metrics emotionally resonant and credible [43-45].
*   **How it works:** **Translate raw data into everyday, tangible contexts** that human beings can readily comprehend (e.g., equating an entire nuclear arsenal to the sound of 5,000 BBs hitting a bucket, or expressing a corporate goal in terms of a soccer team) [44-46].
*   **Trade-offs:** The audience will likely **forget the exact numbers themselves**, retaining only the visceral realization or the underlying relationship they represent [47].

**The "Three Whys"**
*   **When to use:** To bypass the Curse of Knowledge and connect abstract professional tasks to deeper, emotional core values [48, 49].
*   **How it works:** Ask an expert to state the purpose of their idea. Then ask "Why is that important?" **Repeat the question "Why?" at least three times** to force the speaker out of technical jargon and into underlying emotional resonance [48].
*   **Trade-offs:** Can be **frustrating for experts** who feel their initial abstract mission statements are already profound enough [48, 50].

**Mental Simulation (Event Simulation)**
*   **When to use:** To help an audience cope with stress, solve problems, build skills, or prepare for future actions [51-54].
*   **How it works:** Tell a story that forces the audience to **mentally rehearse the step-by-step events, actions, and environmental context** of navigating a problem. This evokes the same physical and emotional brain modules as real physical activity [52, 54, 55].
*   **Trade-offs:** Simulating an event provides **only about two-thirds of the benefit of actual physical practice** [56]. Crucially, simulating *future positive outcomes* (visualizing success) does not improve problem-solving and fails compared to simulating the *process* [57, 58].

**Springboard Stories**
*   **When to use:** To combat skepticism, bypass a hostile "analytical" audience, and mobilize people into an active problem-solving mode [59, 60].
*   **How it works:** Tell a story about how an existing problem was solved on a small scale. This **engages the "little voice inside the head,"** prompting the listener to act as a co-creator and envision how that same solution applies to their own situations [59, 61, 62].
*   **Trade-offs:** Because it does not hit listeners "between the eyes" with an abstract, direct command, some leaders feel it is **too indirect, ambiguous, or anecdotal** [59].

**Fight Sticky with Stickier**
*   **When to use:** To unstick a false rumor, urban legend, or damaging idea [63, 64].
*   **How it works:** Do *not* try to debunk the rumor with logic, denials, or facts, as denying a sticky idea only reinforces it. Instead, **propagate a new, competing message that utilizes the SUCCESs framework and is even *more* sticky** (e.g., McDonald's claiming night crawlers are too expensive to put in burgers), or use a concrete action to shift the turf entirely [65-67].
*   **Trade-offs:** It **requires significant patience, effort, and creativity**. A purely factual defense is easier to draft but less effective, and even successful "stickier" action campaigns (like early auto reliability races) can take years to fully replace the original fear [68, 69].

## Anti-Patterns & Pitfalls

Based on the book *Made to Stick* and our previous discussions of the SUCCESs framework, here are the key anti-patterns and pitfalls that communicators must avoid:

### 1. The Curse of Knowledge
*   **What it is:** A psychological bias where, once you learn something, it becomes nearly impossible to imagine what it feels like *not* to know it [1]. Experts begin to speak in internal "tunes" that sound like incomprehensible taps to their audience [2, 3].
*   **Why it fails:** It leads to using excessive jargon, skipping foundational steps, and communicating abstractly, leaving novices completely confused and unable to act [4-6].
*   **What to do instead:** You cannot unlearn what you know, but you can translate it. Transform your ideas using the SUCCESs checklist (Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories) to bridge the empathy gap between expert and novice [7-9].

### 2. Burying the Lead
*   **What it is:** Getting so steeped in the details and complexities of a topic that you let the most critical element of the message slip too far down in your communication [10-12].
*   **Why it fails:** If your audience loses focus or stops paying attention early, they will completely miss the core point [11, 13]. 
*   **What to do instead:** Practice **forced prioritization**. Use the journalistic "inverted pyramid" structure by putting the most essential information at the very top of your message, with supporting details following in decreasing order of importance [11, 13]. 

### 3. Decision Paralysis
*   **What it is:** Overwhelming an audience with too much complexity, uncertainty, or an abundance of choices (even good choices) without a clear guiding principle [14-16]. 
*   **Why it fails:** Excessive complexity and uncertainty cause anxiety, leading people to act irrationally or simply default to inaction [14, 17, 18].
*   **What to do instead:** Find the core of your message and establish a **"Commander’s Intent"**—a crisp, plain-talk statement of the ultimate goal. This acts as a compass, empowering people to make quick, aligned choices even in complex situations [19-21].

### 4. Gimmicky Surprise (Surprise without Insight)
*   **What it is:** Using shock value or unresolvable puzzles that have no relevance to your core message just to capture attention (e.g., using a pack of wolves in a marching band commercial, or using fake words like "HENSION") [22-24].
*   **Why it fails:** It violates expectations but does not lead to understanding. It comes across as irrelevant, frustrating, and worthless because it doesn't reinforce what you are actually trying to say [23-25].
*   **What to do instead:** Make your surprises **"post-dictable"**. The unexpected twist must break the audience's guessing machines on a relevant issue, but it must make perfect sense in hindsight once the core truth is revealed [26, 27].

### 5. Abstraction Soup
*   **What it is:** Relying on high-level, conceptual language (e.g., "maximizing shareholder value" or "synergistic alignment") instead of grounding ideas in sensory reality [28-30]. 
*   **Why it fails:** Abstraction is the luxury of the expert. For novices, it is difficult to understand, hard to remember, and nearly impossible to coordinate action around because abstract terms mean different things to different people [31-34].
*   **What to do instead:** Use **concrete language**. Translate abstract metrics or strategies into tangible human-scale contexts, specific people doing specific actions, or sensory "hooks" (e.g., a "V8 engine" or a "room filled with 5,000 bouncing BBs") [35-37].

### 6. The "Analytical Hat" (The Drop in the Bucket Effect)
*   **What it is:** Trying to make people care about a cause by bombarding them with massive statistics and the overwhelming scale of a problem [38, 39].
*   **Why it fails:** Thinking about statistics shifts people into an analytical frame of mind. Research proves that the mere act of calculation dampens the emotional centers of the brain, actively reducing human empathy and charitable action [40-42].
*   **What to do instead:** Use the **Mother Teresa Principle**. People are wired to feel empathy for a single, identifiable individual rather than an abstract mass. Focus your message on the story of one specific person [42-44].

### 7. Semantic Stretch
*   **What it is:** Overusing a specific term or concept just because it delivers a strong emotional kick (e.g., calling every minor occurrence "unique", "awesome", or "sportsmanship") [45, 46].
*   **Why it fails:** Like an emotional arms race, overusing terms dilutes their underlying meaning and power over time, rendering them completely ineffective at motivating behavior [45, 47, 48].
*   **What to do instead:** Shift to new turf to reboot the emotional connection. For example, instead of relying on the exhausted term "sportsmanship," the Positive Coaching Alliance created a fresh, highly motivating concept: "Honoring the Game" [49-51].

### 8. Living in "Maslow's Basement"
*   **What it is:** The false psychological assumption that while *we* are motivated by higher-level needs (learning, aesthetics, self-actualization), *everyone else* is primarily motivated by base needs (money, physical security) [52-54]. 
*   **Why it fails:** It severely limits your motivational palette. Bribing people with trivial base rewards (like offering heroic firefighters a popcorn popper for watching a safety film) can insult their sense of identity and backfire [54-56].
*   **What to do instead:** Get out of the basement. Appeal to higher ideals, group identity, and profound self-interest. Inspire people by asking them to embody their ideal self-image (e.g., an army cook declaring, "I am in charge of morale") [57-59].

### 9. Attempting to "Unstick" a False Idea
*   **What it is:** Trying to combat a sticky, false rumor (like "McDonald's uses earthworms in burgers") by directly debunking it with logic, facts, or corporate denials ("unfounded and unsubstantiated") [60, 61]. 
*   **Why it fails:** Denying a sticky idea only reinforces it in the listener's memory. The factual denial is far less memorable than the vivid, shocking rumor [61].
*   **What to do instead:** **Fight sticky with stickier**. You cannot unstick an idea; you must shift the turf by propagating a new, competing message that is even stickier (e.g., "Night crawlers cost six dollars a pound, we can't afford to grind them into meat!"), or use a concrete action to prove your point [62, 63]
